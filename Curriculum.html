QHSE

Two weeks after ISO 9001, the QHSE training cycle continued. The second session brought Mr. Ali Ouni back to deliver a 2-day professional training on ISO 14001:2015 — the international standard for Environmental Management Systems.

The training covered the full structure of the standard: organizational context, interested parties, environmental aspects and impacts, legal compliance obligations, objectives and planning, operational control, and Chapter 9 — Performance Evaluation — covering surveillance, measurement, analysis, and internal audit methodology. This is the same framework used by certified industrial facilities worldwide to manage their environmental footprint.

At the end of the training, I received a professional attestation directly from Mr. Ali Ouni — a formal certificate of completion signed by the trainer himself. As the organizer and participant, that certificate represented something beyond a training session: it was proof that the standard we taught was one we took seriously enough to be evaluated on ourselves.

Formation Révision Moteur — Complete Engine Overhaul Training

Engineering Geeks Club (EGC) — 20+ Hour Practical Workshop, March 7–8, 2020

Technical Training

An automation engineer who understands only electronics is half an engineer. Machines move because of mechanics — and in March 2020, I committed 20+ hours to understanding exactly how the most complex mechanical system in everyday life works: the internal combustion engine.

The Formation Révision Moteur, organized by the Engineering Geeks Club, was a full hands-on overhaul training — starting with theory: thermodynamic cycles, P-V diagrams, combustion physics, valve timing, and the precise role of every bolt and component. Then came the real work: a complete disassembly of a car engine — cylinder head, pistons, connecting rods, crankshaft, timing belt, camshaft, oil pump — everything laid out, identified, and explained one piece at a time.

Then we put it all back together. In the correct order. With the correct torque. And started it. Successfully. That moment — hearing the engine fire up after we had reduced it to individual parts — is one of the most satisfying things I have experienced as an engineering student. It confirmed something important: the best automation engineers are not afraid to get their hands dirty.

Engine Mechanics Mechanical Maintenance System Disassembly & Reassembly Internal Combustion Theory Thermodynamic Cycles Valve Train & Camshaft Torque & Bolt Sequencing Fault Diagnosis Preventive Maintenance
  • Completed a 20+ hour practical training on vehicle engine overhaul — covering theory (thermodynamic cycles, P-V diagrams, combustion physics) and full hands-on disassembly/reassembly of a real car engine.
  • Performed a complete engine teardown — removing and identifying every component: cylinder head, pistons, connecting rods, crankshaft, timing belt, camshaft, oil pump, and all associated hardware in correct sequence.
  • Learned the function, position, and assembly order of every engine component — including torque specifications, bolt sequencing, and gasket placement critical to a successful rebuild.
  • Participated in the full engine reassembly and successful cold start — confirming correct mechanical fit, valve timing, and oil circulation before the engine fired.
  • Passed a theoretical assessment at the end of the training — validating understanding of engine operation principles and maintenance procedures.

ENIS Robots 3.0 — Upgraded, Homologated & Back in the Arena

Engineering Geeks Club (EGC) — National Robotics Competition, Sfax

Competition

Two months after ENICarthage, we were back — but not with the same robot. Every competition teaches you something, and what ENICarthage taught us was clear: the robot needed to do more than move. ENIS Robots 3.0 had strict homologation requirements, and to qualify, our robot had to be capable of picking up an object from a 15 cm elevation and delivering it precisely to unlock a gate — then continue navigating an obstacle course to the finish.

So we engineered an upgrade: a servo-actuated elevator gripper arm mounted on the front of the chassis — controllable in real time, precise enough to grab a target object and release it on command. The robot also got a structural rebuild: reinforced aluminum chassis panels, blue LED headlights for visibility, and cleaner internal wiring to survive the abuse of competition terrain.

The challenge itself was a timed run — not just obstacle avoidance, but a full sequence: approach the elevated object, grip it, lower it, deliver it to the gate trigger, watch the gate open, then race through the remaining course before time runs out. Every second counted. Every mechanism had to work on the first try. That's the kind of pressure that turns builders into engineers.

First Contact with Industrial Automation

ISET Sidi Bouzid — Festo MPS Pneumatic & PLC Training Lab

University Lab

Before any simulation software, before TIA Portal on a laptop — there was this: a real Festo Modular Production System bolted to a lab bench, hissing with compressed air and alive with sensor signals. This is where my hands first touched industrial automation.

The task was to program and control a Pick & Place station — built from pneumatic cylinders, solenoid valves, pressure regulators, and proximity sensors, all coordinated through a PLC running Siemens STEP 7 and programmed in Grafcet (SFC). The station had to pick a workpiece, transfer it via a gantry-style XY axis, and place it precisely at a target position — entirely autonomously.

It sounds straightforward — until you're staring at 40+ pneumatic fittings and a control panel that demands you understand every I/O address before the first cylinder extends. That pressure — the real kind — taught me what no simulation ever could: how to read a system before you dare command it.

Siemens STEP 7 Grafcet / SFC Ladder Logic Pneumatic Actuators Solenoid Valves Proximity Sensors Festo MPS I/O Wiring Pressure Regulation
  • Programmed a full Pick & Place sequence using Grafcet (SFC) — coordinating pneumatic cylinder extensions, gripper actuation, and sensor feedback in a safe sequential flow.
  • Wired and validated all digital I/O connections between the PLC and Festo MPS station, mapping physical sensor/actuator addresses to STEP 7 symbol table entries.
  • Understood pneumatic circuit logic: air supply management, pressure regulation, directional control valves, and double-acting cylinder behavior under load.
  • Debugged real timing conflicts between actuator strokes and sensor feedback — a critical skill that simulation software never exposes.
  • First hands-on exposure to industrial control philosophy: fail-safe design, emergency stop logic, and controlled state transitions.
