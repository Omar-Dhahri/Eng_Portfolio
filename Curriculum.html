🏆 2nd Place — Promise Regionalisation Hackathon — IoT Smart Irrigation System

Promise Regional Program (Tunisia–USA) · Gabes, Gafsa, Medenine & Tataouine — January 2024

IoT · Innovation · 🏆 Award

The brief was simple: 24 hours, one idea, one working prototype. The problem we chose was anything but simple — water waste in agricultural irrigation in a country where drought is not a future threat but a present reality. Tunisia loses enormous quantities of water to inefficient irrigation: fields flooded when they're already saturated, pumps running when no crop needs them, no real-time visibility for farmers about soil conditions. Our team of engineers set out to change that in one night.

I led the IoT hardware and automation layer. While teammates designed the mobile application and shaped the business concept, I built a physical device that read soil moisture, temperature, and water flow data through sensors, processed it through a C++ program running on a microcontroller, and sent real-time readings to a cloud database. The app pulled from that database to give farmers a live dashboard of their field conditions on their phones. But the system didn't just inform — it also acted autonomously: when sensor values crossed defined thresholds, the device triggered automated irrigation responses without waiting for human input. Inform and act. That was the architecture.

By the time we walked onto the stage, we had a working demo of the device, a live data feed to the app, and a video of a real prototype with half its functions operational. The jury awarded us 2nd place at the national level — competing against teams from Gabes, Gafsa, Medenine and Tataouine under the Promise Regional Program, a Tunisian–American initiative supporting innovation in the startup ecosystem. Twenty-four hours. One problem. One solution that worked.

Training Assistant — "Build & Fly" Glider Workshop

Club Aéronautique, Faculté des Sciences de Gafsa — September 2023

Aeronautics · Training

Not every engineering lesson happens in front of a control cabinet. This one happened outside, under an open sky, watching a hand-built glider trace a long, slow arc through the Gafsa air. The Club Aéronautique invited me to assist in a training session with one clear challenge: build a glider from scratch, and make it fly as long as possible.

The session started in the classroom — covering the theoretical foundations that make fixed-wing flight possible: lift, drag, thrust, gravity, and the critical concept of the centre of gravity. Participants learned why a wing generates lift, how the position of the CG determines whether a glider will climb, stall, or dive, and why the choice of materials and geometry directly affects glide ratio. The electric motor theory session covered brushless motors — the motor type used in RC aircraft — explaining the stator/rotor principle, speed-to-weight ratio, and why brushless drives dominate in aerospace applications.

Then the tables were cleared and the real work began. Participants were guided through cutting, measuring, and assembling their glider airframes — balsa and foam shapes transformed into flying wings with ruler, cutter, and careful hands. The final act was a gliding duration competition: each team launched their aircraft and the longest sustained flight won. Engineering stopped being abstract the moment the gliders left their hands — every miscalculated centre of gravity, every asymmetric wing, showed up immediately in the air.

Trainer — "Smart Home" Embedded Systems Workshop

Club Aéronautique, Faculté des Sciences de Gafsa — February 11–12, 2023

Training · IoT · Embedded

About six months after graduating, I wasn't looking for the next thing to learn — I was looking for the next thing to teach. The Club Aéronautique of the Faculté des Sciences de Gafsa gave me that stage. Together with my assistant, I designed and delivered a 2-day hands-on workshop on embedded systems and Smart Home fundamentals, open to over 30 students with no prior hardware experience.

The curriculum was built from first principles: what is a processor, what are sensors and actuators, how does a microcontroller read the physical world and respond to it. From there we moved to the workbench — every participant got hands-on with Arduino Uno and Mega boards, wiring up sensors one by one: ultrasonic distance, infrared, gas, and DHT temperature-humidity. For each sensor, the method was the same — understand the signal type (analog vs. digital), build the mounting circuit, write a dedicated reading function, then nest that function inside a larger program. Outputs followed the same logic: driving an LCD display, triggering a buzzer, controlling a servo motor via a dedicated shield, and finally cutting the wire entirely with a Bluetooth module to complete the Smart Home concept.

Standing at the front of that amphitheatre, explaining to 30 people how to make a sensor talk to a microcontroller, I realized that teaching and engineering are not separate things — explaining a concept clearly enough for someone else to build it is the deepest form of understanding it yourself.

Graduation Internship — Maintenance Management & Production Line Study at Les Ciments de Bizerte

Société des Ciments de Bizerte (SCB) — Baie de Seba Plant, April–October 2022

Maintenance · Automation

My graduation project took me inside one of Tunisia's largest cement production facilities — Les Ciments de Bizerte — for a 6-month deep dive into industrial maintenance management. The mission was clear but challenging: the existing maintenance feedback system generated data with no structure, no KPIs, and no path toward scientific analysis. Breakdowns were recorded, but causes were never linked to effects. There was no foundation for DEMIAC — the structured failure analysis methodology that turns raw maintenance history into actionable decisions.

The first step was to redesign the feedback system from scratch — building a new form structure that connected every failure to its machine, its cause category, its consequence, and its corrective action. This new system was piloted across the ligne d'ensachage et expédition — the cement bagging and dispatch line — which I had reorganized into 5 functional sectors: storage, feeding, bagging, palletizing, and expedition. Each sector was mapped, its machines inventoried, and their datasheets studied. After several months of data collection under the new system, enough history existed to extract real failure patterns and apply targeted maintenance strategies per machine family.

But this internship was never just about paperwork. Alongside the system redesign, I worked directly with the maintenance team on the floor — measuring worn components with calipers, diagnosing toothed belt and rack degradation on the BEUMER robotic palletizer, replacing failed bearings on screw conveyors and bucket elevators, and opening up the Haver & Boecker Roto-Packer's MEC III control system to understand its A/D, CPU, and I/O modules. From a Pepperl+Fuchs AS-i field programmer to a 24V DIN rail power supply swap — every intervention was both a maintenance action and a lesson in how automation, mechanics, and reliability engineering intersect on a real industrial line.

Formation Révision Moteur — Complete Engine Overhaul Training

Engineering Geeks Club (EGC) — 20+ Hour Practical Workshop, March 7–8, 2020

Technical Training

An automation engineer who understands only electronics is half an engineer. Machines move because of mechanics — and in March 2020, I committed 20+ hours to understanding exactly how the most complex mechanical system in everyday life works: the internal combustion engine.

The Formation Révision Moteur, organized by the Engineering Geeks Club, was a full hands-on overhaul training — starting with theory: thermodynamic cycles, P-V diagrams, combustion physics, valve timing, and the precise role of every bolt and component. Then came the real work: a complete disassembly of a car engine — cylinder head, pistons, connecting rods, crankshaft, timing belt, camshaft, oil pump — everything laid out, identified, and explained one piece at a time.

Then we put it all back together. In the correct order. With the correct torque. And started it. Successfully. That moment — hearing the engine fire up after we had reduced it to individual parts — is one of the most satisfying things I have experienced as an engineering student. It confirmed something important: the best automation engineers are not afraid to get their hands dirty.

First Hackathon — SBZ Creathon 2018 — Smart City Emergency & Security System

Association RAWAFED · ISET Sidi Bouzid — October 19–21, 2018 · 28h non-stop

IoT · Smart City · Hackathon

Every journey has a starting point. Mine, in the world of competitive innovation, was a cold October night at ISET Sidi Bouzid — 28 hours, 7 teams, and one challenge: design a sustainability project for the municipality. The jury was not a panel of students. It was the municipality president, the university director, and the event organisers — people who would ask hard questions about feasibility, budget, and real-world impact.

Our team's answer was CITY — a smart city security and emergency response concept built around IoT, GPS positioning, and a city-wide map mesh. The idea was rooted in a real and underaddressed problem: undeclared accidents, medical emergencies on the street, and slow response times in urban areas. Our system proposed placing connected sensors and nodes across the city to detect anomalies — a person down, a crime in progress, an unattended accident — and route the alert to the nearest emergency service automatically, cutting response time dramatically. The citizen becomes both a data source and a beneficiary of the network. We backed it up with a mobile application wireframe showing the city map interface and the alert routing logic.

We didn't win. The challenge was vast, the competition fierce, and we were students building our first serious prototype concept under pressure. But walking out of that 28-hour sprint, I understood something that no classroom had taught me: the best way to learn what you don't know is to commit to solving a problem that requires it. That first hackathon planted a seed that would grow — through workshops, internships, and eventually a national podium finish — over the next six years.

First Contact with Industrial Automation

ISET Sidi Bouzid — Festo MPS Pneumatic & PLC Training Lab

University Lab

Before any simulation software, before TIA Portal on a laptop — there was this: a real Festo Modular Production System bolted to a lab bench, hissing with compressed air and alive with sensor signals. This is where my hands first touched industrial automation.

The task was to program and control a Pick & Place station — built from pneumatic cylinders, solenoid valves, pressure regulators, and proximity sensors, all coordinated through a PLC running Siemens STEP 7 and programmed in Grafcet (SFC). The station had to pick a workpiece, transfer it via a gantry-style XY axis, and place it precisely at a target position — entirely autonomously.

It sounds straightforward — until you're staring at 40+ pneumatic fittings and a control panel that demands you understand every I/O address before the first cylinder extends. That pressure — the real kind — taught me what no simulation ever could: how to read a system before you dare command it.

Siemens STEP 7 Grafcet / SFC Ladder Logic Pneumatic Actuators Solenoid Valves Proximity Sensors Festo MPS I/O Wiring Pressure Regulation
  • Programmed a full Pick & Place sequence using Grafcet (SFC) — coordinating pneumatic cylinder extensions, gripper actuation, and sensor feedback in a safe sequential flow.
  • Wired and validated all digital I/O connections between the PLC and Festo MPS station, mapping physical sensor/actuator addresses to STEP 7 symbol table entries.
  • Understood pneumatic circuit logic: air supply management, pressure regulation, directional control valves, and double-acting cylinder behavior under load.
  • Debugged real timing conflicts between actuator strokes and sensor feedback — a critical skill that simulation software never exposes.
  • First hands-on exposure to industrial control philosophy: fail-safe design, emergency stop logic, and controlled state transitions.
